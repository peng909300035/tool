Why is my PNG bigger than my JPG?

The same photo can be ten times larger as a PNG. What causes the difference, and how to choose a format without losing what you need.

Advertisement

Save the same photo as a PNG and as a JPG, then compare the file sizes. The PNG might be 8 MB while the JPG is 700 KB. The image looks identical, so where did all that weight come from?

PNG keeps everything

PNG is lossless: every pixel is stored exactly, gradients and all. A photo has millions of slightly different colors and PNG accounts for every one of them. That works great for a logo with three flat colors, and badly for a landscape.

JPG throws some of it away

JPG groups nearby pixels and discards detail the eye is unlikely to notice. At quality 80 most people can't tell the difference at normal viewing size. The file is smaller because a lot of that data is simply gone, not because it was packed better.

WebP sits in the middle

WebP supports lossy compression and transparency, and is usually smaller than the equivalent JPG or PNG. Every modern browser reads it. The only snag is older software and the odd upload form that still only takes JPG or PNG.

Converting locally

You don't need to upload the photo to change its format. Our image converter reads the file, draws it on a canvas and exports it in the format you pick. The original metadata gets dropped along the way, which is usually what you want before sharing anyway.

A mistake worth avoiding

People save a photo as PNG because it sounds like the higher quality format, then wonder why a 12 MB file won't upload anywhere. Keep a PNG master if you plan to edit it later, but publish a JPG or WebP copy. Converting a JPG to PNG won't make it sharper, either — the detail was gone the moment it became a JPG.

When PNG is still the right call

Screenshots with text, icons, diagrams, anything with hard edges or real transparency. For those the larger file is a fair trade. Photos go out as JPG or WebP; graphics stay PNG.
